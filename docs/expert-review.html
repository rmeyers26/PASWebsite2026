Executive summary

The engineering is now better than the content it serves.

The SpaceHero component is the real thing — a procedurally-generated three.js scene with a limb-darkened Saturn, a correctly-inclined Andromeda, GPU-shader star twinkle, a frustum-relative composition that recomposes for portrait, idle-deferred loading behind a CSS fallback, and a reduced-motion tier that keeps ambient motion while cutting input-driven parallax. That is craft at the level of the companies in the comparison set.

The design tokens are equally serious. The stylesheet contains a comment computing that rgba(255,255,255,0.24) only reaches 2.1:1 against #0a0e17 and therefore raising it to 0.34 to satisfy WCAG 1.4.11. Almost nobody does that.

Then you scroll, and the site stops being that.

Below the fold, every page resolves into the same card grid. Eight amber warning banners reading "⚠ Placeholder content — needs real info from PAS" are visible to the public across seven of fourteen pages. The primary conversion path — a "Join PAS" button in the header on every page, plus two more on the homepage — terminates on a page that says online signup is "coming in a future phase" and redirects to a list of mailto: links.

Membership dues are never stated. The meeting time is never stated. The street address is never stated. There is no map. There is no newsletter. There are no social links of any kind. There is not one photograph taken through a member's telescope.

The finding that should sting

This is an astronomy club. Its members own the most emotionally powerful raw material any organization in this category can have — their own images of the sky. The site instead borrows NASA's Astronomy Picture of the Day, dumps the unedited NASA caption into a card (including NASA's own promotional line, "What picture did APOD feature on your birthday?"), and calls that the wonder section. The club's own visual identity is a handful of undirected phone snapshots of people at folding tables under fluorescent light.

Nothing on the critical path is a rebuild. This is a launch-blocking content and photography problem wearing a well-built front end.

Part 1 · First impression
8/10

The first five seconds are the best thing here.

At 1440px the hero is genuinely arresting. Saturn sits upper-right with a properly UV-remapped ring and limb darkening that makes it read as a sphere rather than a decal. Andromeda occupies the upper-left as 6,000 additively-blended points. Stars twinkle on independent per-vertex phases. The type is large, confident, and correctly seated on a radial scrim so it never fights a bright star. Nothing here looks like a template.

Would I immediately stay? Yes.
Does this feel premium? The hero does.
Does this look expensive? Yes — until you scroll.
Custom or template-based? Unmistakably custom.
Does it feel alive? Yes. The twinkle is time-driven, not hover-driven, so it plays on touch devices too.
Clarity of purpose? Weak.

The clarity failure is the one thing dragging this off a 9. Nothing above the fold says free, says Phoenix, says no telescope required, or says the next public star party is on the 14th. The single most persuasive fact this club owns — anyone can turn up, for free, tonight, and look through a real telescope — is buried in a card three screens down. The hero is selling atmosphere when it could be selling an invitation.

Two real flaws

  1. The moon is a bug that looks like a smudge. At MOON_DEPTH = 4 it renders as a near-black disc with no visible craters and no terminator. On the desktop capture it reads as dust on the monitor. Either light it so its 150-crater procedural texture earns its place, or remove it.
  2. Portrait composition collides with the CTA. At 390×844, Saturn's ring passes directly through the "Upcoming Events" button, and the eyebrow badge — text-xs at tracking-[0.2em], nearly full viewport width — sits on top of the galaxy's arm. The portrait composition object clears the vertical middle for the headline but never accounts for the CTA slot below the subtitle.
Part 2 · Branding
55/100

There is a visual style here. There is not yet a visual identity.

Logo

Used at 36×36 in the header and as the favicon. It is a detailed circular badge with a telescope silhouette and small type — at 36px it is illegible mush, and it is the only place the brand mark appears on the entire site. It is also the Open Graph image for all fourteen pages, so every share of this site anywhere renders as a tiny logo on a card.

Typography

Space Grotesk over Inter, competently set — the seven-step fluid --text-d1…d7 scale with clamp() is better type infrastructure than most sites in the comparison set have. But these are the two most-defaulted-to typefaces of the last five years. They say "this is a modern website" rather than "this is the Phoenix Astronomical Society." A club with a 1948 founding date, a lost observatory project, and a Tombaugh-autographed poster in its archive has a typographic story available to it that Space Grotesk does not tell.

Color

Violet #8b5cf6 / teal #2dd4bf / amber #f5b942 on #0a0e17. Well-executed — accents are assigned semantically per card and the amber CTA hits 10.85:1 — but violet-and-teal-on-dark is what every astronomy site, every SaaS dark mode, and every AI-generated "space" comp looks like in 2026. Nothing here is Arizona. Nothing is Sonoran. This site is set in a desert with the most distinctive sky and light quality in the continental United States, and the palette could belong to a crypto exchange.

Photography

The critical failure. Every image is an undirected snapshot — fluorescent-lit meeting rooms, a man standing in a beige lot, a crowd at dusk. Honest and human, which has value, but ungraded, unretouched, inconsistent in color temperature, and the only imagery the brand owns.

Could another club copy it? The hero, no — that is real engineering. Everything else, yes, in a weekend, by swapping the logo. What emotion does the brand create? Competence. Not wonder, not welcome, not belonging.

Part 3 · Visual design
78/100

Genuinely good craft, monotonously applied.

What is excellent

  • The token system. --color-subtle (0.1 alpha, decorative rules) versus --color-strong (0.34 alpha, interactive boundaries) is a distinction most design systems never draw, and the stylesheet documents why with the contrast math.
  • Card hierarchy. .card-featured exists specifically so one item per grid becomes a focal point "instead of reading as an undifferentiated matrix."
  • Depth is restrained. An inset top highlight plus a low-opacity drop shadow, escalating to a violet-tinted glow on hover. Glass without the 2021 glassmorphism cliché.
  • Vertical rhythm. Consistent py-20 sections with hairline separators and alternating surface tint, across all fourteen pages.
  • text-wrap: balance on headings and text-wrap: pretty on paragraphs. Small, correct, rarely done.

What breaks it

  1. .text-gradient is applied to headings too short to hold a gradient. On the press page, "Press Releases" renders with "Press" in near-white and "Releases" in violet-to-teal. It reads as two different emphases rather than one heading. Same on /history, /leadership, /contact, /404.
  2. The card grid is the only compositional idea below the fold. Homepage: 3-up cards, photo band, 6-up cards, 2-up cards. Exoplanets: 2-up, 2-up. Learn: 2-up, 3-up. No long-form layout, no editorial spread, no data visualization. The monotony is invisible on any single page and obvious across the site.
  3. The breadcrumb bar creates an orphaned strip. On every interior page a thin band of starfield sits between the sticky header and the hero image. On the homepage there is no breadcrumb and the hero touches the header. Two structural treatments for one slot.
  4. Alignment inconsistency. Four pages open with a centered text hero over nothing; the rest open with a centered hero over a photo. The first variant reads as an unfinished version of the second.
Part 4 · Hero section
9/10 · 4/10

There are two heroes on this site and they are not in the same league.

SpaceHero.astro (homepage only) is world-class. Hero.astro (eight interior pages) is where the site loses most of its perceived quality, and the cause is a single line:

class="absolute inset-0 h-full w-full object-cover opacity-60"

Every hero image, regardless of source, is set to 60% opacity and then covered with a scrim. Applied uniformly, this does two incompatible things:

  • Night photographs die. /events uses a telescope-beside-fireworks night shot. At 60% opacity under a 70% scrim it is functionally black — in the desktop capture you cannot identify the telescope, the fireworks, or the fence. The most exciting photograph in the library renders as a dark rectangle.
  • Daytime photographs survive and collide. /learn uses a daytime shot of a member beside a refractor. It survives — and the headline "Start Exploring the Sky" lands directly across the man's face. No focal-point offset, no per-image crop.
Worst single asset decision on the site

The /join hero. The page whose entire job is to make someone want to belong opens with a fluorescent-lit community-college classroom of people seated at folding tables. It is a photograph of a meeting. Nobody has ever joined an astronomy club because they wanted to attend a meeting.

Seven of the eight interior heroes contain no call to action. /events asks "When Is the Next Observing Session?" in 80px type and then provides no answer and no button. /join states "Become Part of the PAS Community" and offers nothing to click.

Part 5 · Navigation
84/100

The most professional part of the site after the hero.

Four grouped top-level items plus a persistent amber "Join PAS" CTA. Fourteen pages organized into five slots is disciplined information architecture.

Done right

  • Built on <details>/<summary>, so it is keyboard-operable and fully functional with JavaScript disabled — the script only adds behaviors the element lacks.
  • aria-expanded maintained on toggle; aria-current="page" on both the item and its parent group; Escape closes and returns focus to the summary; outside-click closes; one panel open at a time.
  • Document-level listeners registered once and re-querying on each event, so they survive view transitions — a bug class most implementations ship.
  • The trailing-slash normalization is a real bug that was found and fixed; without it no nav item would ever highlight on a statically-built subpage.

Gaps

  1. No search. With 14 pages, a Bortle-rated site list, seasonal target tables by aperture, a press archive back to 2021, and a long-form history, search is now justified.
  2. The footer is a bare link list. No sitemap grouping, no social, no newsletter, no address, no next event, no donation link. For a nonprofit the footer is prime real estate and it is empty.
  3. aria-haspopup is absent on the dropdown summaries. Minor, but free.
  4. The mobile menu offers no route to the homepage except the logo.
Part 6 · User experience
62/100

The membership journey is a dead end.

This is the most important finding in this document.

Header "Join PAS" (on all 14 pages) Homepage "Join the Club" (hero) Homepage "Join the Club" (Why Join) Homepage "Become a Member" (SIG section) ↓ /join ↓ "Online membership signup is coming in a future phase of this site. For now, reach out and a PAS officer will help you get started." ↓ /contact ↓ mailto:info@pasaz.org ↓ (the visitor's mail client, and then nothing)

Four distinct calls to action converge on a page that admits it cannot do the thing it exists to do. Along the way the site never states what membership costs, what tiers exist, what you get, when meetings are, or where they are. /join lists four benefits, three of which — access to star parties, monthly meetings, a community of mentors — are things the site elsewhere says are free and open to the public without membership.

Root cause

The page cannot answer "why would I pay for this?" because the site never says there is anything to pay.

Event discovery is nearly as bad

The homepage section titled "Upcoming Events" contains no events. It contains three cards describing event types — "Monthly Meeting," "Public Star Party," "Telescope Workshop" — with no dates. The button reads "View Full Calendar" and leads to /events, whose hero asks "When Is the Next Observing Session?" and whose answer is an embedded Bookwhen <iframe>.

That iframe is the only place on the entire site where a date lives. It is third-party, below the fold, lazily loaded, not indexable, and inherits none of the site's typography or color. During this review the embed failed to load and the page offered the visitor nothing.

A visitor cannot learn when the next public star party is without leaving the site. For a club whose outreach mission depends on people showing up on a specific night, this is the highest-leverage defect here.

Friction

Cognitive load is low — plain writing, short pages, scannable grids. The friction is structural: every path that matters ends in mailto:. Star Tours booking, volunteering, joining, contact. There is not a single <form> element in the codebase.

Delight

The parallax photo band on the homepage — driven by CSS animation-timeline: view() with no scroll listener and no JavaScript, gated behind @supports and prefers-reduced-motion — is a genuinely tasteful signature moment. The 404 page is charming. That is the complete inventory of delight below the hero.

Part 7 · Mobile
65/100

The hero eats the first screen and delivers no information.

Working

Responsive grids collapse cleanly. The hamburger is a 44×44 target and mobile nav rows clear 44px. The mobile panel is height-capped with overflow-y-auto so it can never exceed the screen. The starfield is dimmed to opacity: 0.45 under 40rem specifically because "discrete bright dots read as dirt rather than atmosphere" behind narrow text columns — a considered, non-obvious call. The fluid type scale gives phones 44px headings rather than 36px.

Broken

  1. At 390×844 the first screen is badge, three lines of headline, four lines of subtitle, two stacked buttons — and the second button sits below the fold behind Saturn's ring. A phone visitor's first screen contains zero facts.
  2. The eyebrow badge does not survive portrait: text-xs at tracking-[0.2em] renders at nearly full viewport width, overlapping the galaxy, pill border pressed to both edges.
  3. Header + breadcrumb + hero consumes roughly 60% of the viewport before any content, on every one of eight interior pages.
  4. Two third-party embeds (Bookwhen, Astrospheric) not designed for a 390px column, over whose touch targets, contrast, and scroll behavior the site has no control.

Would someone enjoy browsing entirely from a phone? They would enjoy the hero, then spend the session scrolling past photography darkened to near-black, unable to find out when anything happens.

Part 8 · Desktop
82/100

Designed at tablet width, centered on desktop.

The hero scales beautifully — min-h-[92svh], correct svh unit choice, canvas sized from section.clientWidth with an rAF-debounced resize handler, and a full scene re-layout on resize rather than a naive camera adjustment.

Content max-widths are disciplined and varied by content type — 3xl for the homepage manifesto, 4xl for prose, 5xl for learn grids, 6xl for card sections, 7xl for the nav. That is a real editorial decision, not one global container.

Where it falls short: at 1440px and above, interior pages are a 4xl column centered in a wide dark field, leaving roughly 60% of the viewport occupied by nothing but the fixed starfield. No sidebar, no marginalia, no pull quote, no wide-format moment. Information density is low throughout — /leadership uses a full-width bordered row per president for 29 presidents, which is a table pretending to be a card list.

Part 9 · Content
72/100

The writing is much better than the content strategy.

Somebody who can write worked on this:

"Phoenix has been looking up since 1948 — and for nearly eight decades, someone from this club has been standing next to the telescope, explaining what you're seeing."

"We're a 501(c)(3) nonprofit of beginners, backyard observers, astrophotographers, and lifelong amateurs. The telescopes are a means to an end; the point is the people around them."

"Most people meet us holding a rock older than the Earth."

That last headline is the best line on the site — specific, surprising, and it earns its photograph. The /learn career FAQ is the most substantive content anywhere here. The /history page is a genuine archival asset: the lost observatory, the mirror auctioned in 1976 and bought by a past president for $2,600, the Tombaugh poster.

What undermines it

  1. Eight public "placeholder" banners. Amber-bordered, amber-backed, impossible to miss, on /, /join, /contact, /events, /learn, /press-releases, and twice on /about. One tells the public that the contact addresses "were found via web research and should be verified." Another tells them the press release PDFs are missing. This is internal project management leaking onto production, and it destroys credibility more thoroughly than anything else here.
  2. The APOD card dumps NASA's raw caption. No clamp, no excerpt, no editorial framing. The August capture runs 250+ words in an unbounded paragraph that unbalances the card and ends with NASA's own site-promotion line — "Sky Surprise: What picture did APOD feature on your birthday?" — an instruction to visit a different website, printed on the PAS homepage.
  3. A text bug ships to production. /press-releases renders "contact us atinfo@pasaz.org" — the space before the link is missing in the built HTML.
  4. /solar-system and /exoplanets are link pages wearing page costumes. The first is 57 lines whose entire purpose is to send the visitor to eyes.nasa.gov. The second is a competent but entirely generic explainer with one sentence of actual PAS relevance. Both are in the primary navigation.
  5. There is no content written for a child on this site. The "Kids Corner" is four links to other organizations' children's sites.

Part 12 · Accessibility
80/100

Strong fundamentals, two real WCAG 2.2 gaps.

Above the industry standard

Contrast is deliberate and documented. Measured: body muted #9ba3b7 on #0a0e17 = 7.65:1; teal links = 10.4:1; amber primary button = 10.85:1. All comfortably exceed AA, most exceed AAA. The --color-strong token exists solely to bring interactive boundaries to 3:1 for SC 1.4.11, with the arithmetic in a comment.

Reduced motion is tiered, not binary. The stylesheet kills the reveal, the starfield drift, the twinkle, and the parallax band; the WebGL hero keeps slow ambient rotation at motionScale = 0.3 but drops mouse parallax, scroll dolly, and shooting stars. The comment reasons correctly that WCAG asks to avoid vestibular-triggering motion, not to freeze the page.

Progressive enhancement is real. Navigation works with JS disabled. The reveal animation's hidden state is applied by script rather than CSS specifically so no-JS visitors see all content. Skip link with a visible focus state; one <h1> per page across all fourteen; correct heading order; descriptive alt text throughout.

The gaps

  1. There is no :focus-visible system. The only custom focus style in the codebase is on the skip link. Every button, card link, nav summary, and dropdown item falls back to the UA default ring. On a near-black ground with pill buttons and translucent glass cards that is inconsistent at best. WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.13 risk.
  2. The sticky header will obscure focused elements. Roughly 69px tall, sticky top-0 z-50. Tabbing scrolls focused elements to the viewport top, directly underneath it. Only /sky-conditions sets scroll-margin-top. WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.11 — a Level AA failure across all fourteen pages.
  3. Bullet lists built from typed • characters inside <li> on /events.
  4. Two third-party iframes whose internal accessibility is outside the site's control, on two of the most important pages.

Estimated automated score: 92–96. Estimated manual audit result: fails AA on 2.4.11, borderline on 2.4.13.

Part 13 · Performance
74/100

Sophisticated loading strategy, undermined by font delivery.

AssetSize

Handled well

  • three.js is dynamically imported inside requestIdleCallback behind a CSS-gradient fallback painted immediately — the hero has a real LCP candidate before any WebGL work begins.
  • The render loop early-returns when the hero is off-screen. Full teardown and renderer.dispose() on view transition and pagehide.
  • All textures generated procedurally on canvas — zero network requests for the entire 3D scene.
  • The APOD component reads JPEG SOF / PNG IHDR markers from a ranged 128KB request at build time purely to obtain intrinsic dimensions and prevent CLS. An unusually thorough solution to a layout-shift problem.
  • The parallax band uses CSS animation-timeline: view() — no scroll listener, no JS.

Costing you

  1. No font preloading. Both variable fonts are discovered only after the 46KB stylesheet parses. Text renders in system-ui and then swaps — a visible FOUT on the largest text on the page, which is also the LCP element.
  2. Ten woff2 files for an English-only site. The bare @fontsource-variable/* imports pull Cyrillic, Cyrillic-ext, Greek, Greek-ext, Vietnamese, and Latin-ext. Importing only /latin.css would cut this to two files.
  3. Two render-blocking third-party scripts on the two most important pages. Bookwhen on /events has no defer and no preconnect; Astrospheric on /sky-conditions adds a polling loop of up to 50 × 100ms.

Projected Core Web Vitals: LCP good on desktop (~1.5–2.0s), borderline on mid-range mobile due to the font swap. CLS excellent. INP good except on /events and /sky-conditions, where third-party embeds dominate.

Part 14 · SEO
62/100

Clean technical hygiene, zero structured data.

Present and correct: unique titles and descriptions per page, canonical URLs, generated sitemap, robots.txt, clean semantic URLs, one <h1> per page, five legacy redirects preserving inbound links, Open Graph and Twitter Card tags everywhere, and static HTML output so everything is crawlable.

The gaps, in order of cost

  1. There is no structured data anywhere on the site. Zero application/ld+json. No Organization, no Event — and therefore no rich results for "star party phoenix," the exact query this club needs to win — no BreadcrumbList despite breadcrumbs being rendered, and no FAQPage on /learn, which contains five well-formed Q&A pairs sitting ready for it.
  2. Every page shares one Open Graph image: the 72px logo PNG. For an organization whose subject matter is pictures of space, this is the most wasteful line in the codebase.
  3. Local SEO is entirely absent. No LocalBusiness schema, no name/address/phone block, no street address anywhere, no map, no Business Profile link. A local club invisible to "astronomy club near me."
  4. The events calendar is inside an iframe, so the highest-intent content on the site is invisible to search engines.
  5. Unclaimed keywords: "star party phoenix," "where to stargaze near phoenix," "dark sky sites arizona," "telescope help phoenix." The Bortle-rated site list is genuinely strong material for these — and it is buried under a nav dropdown labeled "Learn," with no dedicated landing page, no schema, and no descriptive internal anchors.
Part 15 · Motion
80/100

Restraint is the achievement here.

Star twinkle is a GPU vertex/fragment shader with per-star phase and speed attributes. The comment notes it is deliberately time-driven rather than hover-driven "so it plays identically on touch devices with no pointer at all." The fragment shader uses a tight core with fast falloff, with a comment explaining that a linear ramp would read as a blurred disc rather than a star. That is someone who has actually looked at stars.

The parallax band — CSS scroll-driven, @supports-gated, reduced-motion-gated, ±3.5% translate at 1.18 scale — is the correct way to do a scroll effect in 2026. Scroll reveal is understated and unobserved after firing, so it never replays.

Verified defect · three-line fix

The scroll reveal breaks printing. Under print-media emulation, the homepage hero renders at opacity: 1 and all seven sections below it render at opacity: 0. Printing or saving any page to PDF produces a hero followed by blank pages. The .reveal hidden state is applied by script and only removed by an IntersectionObserver that never fires in a non-scrolling render context. This also affects full-page screenshot services.

@media print { .reveal { opacity: 1; transform: none } }

Otherwise: nothing hovers meaningfully except cards and buttons, both of which lift 2px and glow. That is the complete microinteraction inventory. All the motion is in the hero and all of it is ambient — nothing communicates, nothing responds to a decision the user made.

Does motion enhance or distract? Enhance. This is one of the few sites in this category that does not overdo it.

Part 16 · Architecture
85/100

The most professionally-executed dimension.

Astro 7 with Tailwind 4, static output. Exactly right: fourteen mostly-static pages, near-zero client JS by default, islands only where genuinely needed. React or Next.js here would have been a mistake.

The comments explain why, not what, and they document real decisions:

  • Why --color-strong is 0.34 alpha (the contrast math).
  • Why the hero title tail is solid ink rather than gradient (the gradient's violet stop loses contrast on a violet sky).
  • Why the reveal's hidden state lives in JS rather than CSS (no-JS visitors).
  • Why document-level listeners are registered once (they survive view transitions).
  • Why limb darkening is applied to Saturn ("without it, flat banding reads as a beach ball").

View-transition correctness is where most Astro sites with ClientRouter are quietly broken, and this one is not. Teardown on astro:before-swap, cancelled idle callbacks, disposed renderer, disconnected observers, and data-astro-rerun on the inline reveal script with an explanation of why a bundled module would only run once.

Weaknesses

  1. No CMS. Content is hardcoded in .astro files. Every officer name, press release, observing site, and president is a TypeScript array literal inside a page component. A volunteer club officer cannot update the site without editing source and redeploying. This is the single greatest threat to the site's long-term survival — clubs abandon sites they cannot update.
  2. No tests, no CI, no lint or formatter config. On a volunteer-maintained project, this is how regressions ship.
  3. No analytics and no error monitoring. There is no way to know whether anyone reaches /join, or whether the Bookwhen embed is failing for real visitors.
  4. The APOD fetch is build-time only, and no scheduled rebuild is configured — so "Picture of the Day" goes stale.
  5. Two third-party runtime dependencies loaded as plain scripts with no SRI and no CSP.

Part 19 · Honest critique

Direct answers.

What feels dated?

Very little visually. The dated things are structural: mailto: links as the primary conversion mechanism, third-party iframes as core functionality, and hardcoded content arrays with no CMS. Those are 2010 patterns wearing a 2026 skin.

What feels generic?

The violet/teal/dark palette. Space Grotesk + Inter. The card grid, which is the only layout idea below the fold. /exoplanets, which reads like an encyclopedia entry. The four /join bullets. The word "community," used eleven times without ever being demonstrated.

What feels amateur?

  1. Eight amber "⚠ Placeholder content" banners on the live site.
  2. A "Join" page that cannot be joined.
  3. The /join hero: a fluorescent-lit room of people at folding tables.
  4. /learn's headline printed across a man's face.
  5. atinfo@pasaz.org on the press page.
  6. The near-black /events hero.
  7. .text-gradient splitting "Press Releases" into two colors.
  8. The unclamped APOD caption ending in NASA's birthday-picture promo.

What feels premium?

The hero, unreservedly. The token system and its contrast documentation. The reduced-motion tiering. The <details>-based navigation. The parallax band. The APOD dimension-probe for CLS. The type scale. The homepage manifesto copy. "Most people meet us holding a rock older than the Earth."

What should be redesigned from scratch?

  1. Hero.astro. One uniform opacity/scrim treatment cannot serve both night and daytime photography. It needs per-image focal points, per-image scrim direction, left-aligned variants, and a CTA slot.
  2. /join. Rebuild around dues, tiers, a real signup, and one photograph of a person's face lit by a screen at 11pm.
  3. /events. Own the data. Dates in HTML, Event schema, next-three-events on the homepage.

What is missing?

Dates. Prices. An address. A map. A form. A gallery. A newsletter. A social link. A donate button. Speaker names. Member faces. Structured data. Anything a person could share.

What is overdesigned?

Almost nothing — which is a compliment. If pressed: ambient orbs plus a CSS starfield plus a WebGL starfield plus nebula sprites are four simultaneous background systems, and behind long-form pages like /history the fixed starfield adds noise the reading experience does not want.

What disappoints users?

Clicking "Join PAS."

The nine questions

1 · Is this truly a world-class website?

No. It has a world-class component. The hero would not be out of place on a site from any organization in the comparison set. The other 90% of the experience is a well-built, well-written, under-populated club site. World-class is a property of the whole experience, and this experience ends at a "Join" page that cannot be joined.

2 · Would it be competitive for an Awwwards nomination?

The hero alone would draw attention. The submission would not survive judging — Usability would collapse on the dead-end join flow and Content would collapse on eight visible placeholder banners. Honorable mention at the very best; realistically, no.

3 · Would it impress a first-time visitor within five seconds?

Yes, on desktop. Emphatically. On mobile, the first screen contains no facts and the second CTA is behind a planet.

4 · Would it attract younger audiences while serving experienced astronomers?

It serves experienced observers well — /sky-conditions is a genuine resource. It does not attract younger audiences: no social presence, no gallery, no video, no shareable content, no member faces, and a membership page illustrated with a photograph of a meeting.

5 · Would someone be excited enough to become a member after visiting?

They would be excited by the hero and then unable to act on it. There is no price, no signup, no date, no address. The site currently cannot produce a member.

6 · Top 10 changes with the most impact

  1. Delete or fill all eight placeholder banners.
  2. Make /join actually work — dues, tiers, a real form.
  3. Put the next three dated events on the homepage in real HTML.
  4. Build the member astrophotography gallery.
  5. Replace the /join and /events hero images.
  6. Add an address, a map, and meeting times.
  7. Add Event and Organization JSON-LD.
  8. Replace every mailto: flow with a form.
  9. Add a newsletter signup, social links, and a donate button.
  10. Add a :focus-visible system and fix the sticky-header focus obstruction.

Eight of ten are content and information decisions, not code. That is the shape of this project.

Curate and grade 30 member astrophotographs. The highest-return line item on this list, because it is the one thing that cannot be engineered.

8 · With $250,000

Everything above, plus:

  • A distinctive brand identity. A commissioned typeface pairing and a palette derived from actual Sonoran night — the quality of Arizona light at astronomical twilight, the color of a red headlamp on saguaro, the horizon glow of Phoenix seen from Bortle 2. Retire violet-and-teal entirely.
  • "What's Up Over Phoenix" as a real monthly publication with an editor, a template system, and an email program. This is the engine that turns a website into an institution.
  • A member platform — accounts, observation logs, a moderated photo pipeline, badges tied to Astronomical League observing programs.
  • "The Observatory That Was Never Built" — a scroll-driven narrative built from the archive already sitting in /history: Amos Hoff, the 1973 Kaufman plans, the county choosing an equestrian facility with "the usual glaring arena lighting," the mirror auctioned in 1976 and bought back by a past president. A genuinely singular story no other astronomy club on earth can tell, currently presented as a wall of body copy. Done properly, this is the piece that gets written about.
  • A live sky utility the club owns rather than embeds, in its own design language.
  • Educator hub with downloadable, standards-aligned materials.
  • A third-party accessibility audit and a WCAG 2.2 AA conformance statement.

9 · What would I refuse to launch until it was fixed?

  1. Every placeholder banner. Non-negotiable. A public site does not tell visitors it is unfinished.
  2. The join dead end. Either a working signup, or an honest, well-designed "membership is handled by email — here is exactly what to write and what it costs." What ships today is neither.
  3. No dates anywhere in HTML. An events organization whose events are invisible without JavaScript and invisible to search engines entirely.
  4. No address and no map.
  5. The /join hero photograph. I would ship a solid gradient before I would ship that room.
  6. The print/PDF blank-page bug. Three lines. No excuse.
  7. The atinfo@pasaz.org typo.

I would launch with the WCAG 2.4.11 issue open and fix it in week one. I would not launch with the first seven.

Action plan

The smallest set of changes that transforms this site

Roughly three weeks of work. Moves the site from "impressive demo" to "best astronomy club website in the United States."

  1. Delete every placeholder banner — by filling the content, not by hiding the warning.
  2. Put dates on the site. Next three events on the homepage, full list on /events, in real HTML, with Event schema. Add the address and a map.
  3. Make /join work. State the dues. State the tiers. Add a form or a payment link. Replace the conference-room photograph.

  5. Add the missing connective tissue — newsletter, social links, donate, forms instead of mailto:, :focus-visible, and the three-line print fix.

None of this is a redesign. The design is already good and the engineering is already better than good. What this site needs is for the club to show up inside it.
